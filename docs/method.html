Under the hood

How Bihua reads your hand.

And when it brings the character back. Two engines do the real work: a memory model chooses the date, the ink checker reports what your hand produced. The rest of the app serves those jobs, including the places where it refuses to decide for you.

14 min read FSRS-7 · SBD · ML Kit Digital Ink · custom stroke comparison Android 10+ · offline

I built Bihua because I could recognise characters I could not write. A word list would tell me I was right. A blank square told a different story. The app starts from that second answer.

Two rules

Do not show the character before the attempt. Do not let a recognition model choose the grade. Everything else follows from those two rules.

The loop

A new character gets three screens. You meet it, trace it once, then write it from memory. A review skips the first two and starts at the blank square. The canvas is the work; the rest is cue and evidence.

The app does not keep you in the lesson after the recall. It records the grade you choose, schedules the next date, and moves on. Sessions stay short because short sessions are easier to repeat.

Choosing the date: FSRS-7 or SBD

Bihua runs its own FSRS-7 implementation, following the open-spaced-repetition formulas. There are no fixed intervals such as “1 day, 3 days, 7 days.”

Each card keeps three numbers:

  • Long-term stability: how long the memory is expected to survive on the long curve.
  • Short-term stability: the same idea for same-day and next-day reviews.
  • Difficulty: how hard this item tends to be for this learner.

On every review the app estimates retrievability from those numbers and the elapsed time. It then solves for the interval at your target recall, 90% by default and adjustable from 70% to 97%, capped at 365 days. The solver is numerical because the combined forgetting curve does not have a clean inverse.

FSRS-6 used one stability value and rounded elapsed time to whole days. FSRS-7 keeps a second, faster trace and works in fractional days, so a retry ten minutes later is not treated like a retry tomorrow. The cost is complexity: 34 weights instead of 21, and a numerical interval solver.

SBD is a newer, smaller model from A. Schill’s 2026 paper Interpretable Memory Models for Spaced Repetition. It keeps three numbers per card and seven weights in total: a stability ladder, a grade index, and a count of successful reviews. The published defaults work immediately, and the weights can be refitted from your own reviews on the device. In the public benchmark it lands close to FSRS-7: slightly higher log loss, slightly lower RMSE, with a fraction of the parameters.

Both models keep a current memory state on every card. Choosing one does not erase your history, and switching in Settings takes effect on the next review. The target-recall slider applies to both. Lower targets mean longer gaps and fewer reviews; higher targets mean stronger recall and more work. Bihua recommends 90% for daily study.

Bihua ships the published default weights. It does not fit a personal model from your review history yet.

The grade is the input

Again, Hard, Good, Easy. The ink checker can suggest one, but it never submits. The scheduler only knows what you tell it. That is a feature: recognition is probabilistic, and the learner is the one who saw the attempt.

Same-day reviews use the short-term curve. A lapse lowers stability and raises difficulty; a success raises stability and adjusts difficulty. This is why pressing Again does not simply reset a box. It changes the model.

Vacation mode pauses reviews and new cards, then shifts due dates when it ends. The next seven days cap a session at ten cards. The pause is not free; FSRS is happiest without long gaps. The cap is there to make the return survivable.

The ink checker: what the hand did

There are two layers, and they answer different questions.

1. What might this be?

ML Kit Digital Ink runs on the device and returns candidate characters. The model is selected for the script and the target: zh-Hans, zh-Hant, ja, or ko. Korean Hanja routes to a Han model; Hangul routes to the Korean model.

2. How close is it to the guide?

Bihua compares the drawn strokes with the reference centerlines. It reports stroke count, average distance from the guide, reversed strokes, strokes out of order, missing or extra strokes, and the first correction worth trying. Stylus pressure is averaged and eased; a finger or mouse draws at a fixed width.

The first layer is a downloaded local model. The second is deterministic code. Neither one is allowed to press the grade button.

When the attempt goes wrong, the correction pad can isolate one stroke and play it again. The learner can undo, clear, redraw, or reveal the guide. The point is to give the next attempt a target, not to produce a perfect score.

What it cannot do

Some rare glyphs have no open stroke guide. Recognition can be wrong, especially with unusual handwriting. Korean Hanja is not a separate recognition language, so it borrows a Han model. The app says when it has no evidence instead of inventing a verdict.

Why write first

Retrieval practice is the part of study that feels like work. Reading a character and nodding is recognition; producing it from a cue is retrieval. The second one leaves a different trace, especially when the response is a sequence of strokes rather than a tap.

Handwriting adds motor planning and spatial sequencing to the task. That does not make it magic, and it does not replace reading practice. It makes writing a separate skill that deserves its own evidence. Bihua keeps that evidence in front of you before it shows the answer.

If the character is already on the screen, the hand is copying. Copying is useful on the first pass. It is not a review.

Fading guides

A guide that never goes away teaches tracing. Bihua starts with full strokes, moves to an outline after a few reviews, then hides the reference. The learner can always ask for it, but recall starts without it.

The fade is tied to review count, not to a timer. A character you have written once does not need the same scaffold as one you have written ten times.

Two tasks

Writing and pronunciation are tested separately. A review asks for one skill, not a blended score. If you can write 愛 but cannot say it, the app should show that gap rather than average it away.

The pronunciation task accepts pinyin, romaji or kana, and Hangul or romaja. A small live preview composes Hangul from Latin input so a learner without a Korean keyboard can still see the word take shape. It checks the reading the learner typed and shows a suggestion. The learner still grades. Neither task schedules the other.

Content choices

The character list is not a word list. Simplified and Traditional are separate courses, and shared glyphs stay in both. Traditional words come from CEDICT word-level pairs, because a character-by-character substitution produces forms like 頭發 instead of 頭髮.

The Korean course starts with Hangul, then the Hanja education list from Unihan: readings and definitions. Korean word examples come from the Korean Wiktionary extract, ranked by the FrequencyWords list. Words with only explicit dictionary senses are tagged and stay hidden until the learner turns on Mature words.

Chinese stroke data comes from Make Me a Hanzi; Japanese data from KanjiVG; definitions and readings from CC-CEDICT, KANJIDIC2, and JMdict. The notices and full licences ship with the app and the repository.

Tradeoffs

There are no streaks, coins, leaderboards, or loss-framed reminders. Those mechanics can bring people back, but they also make a missed day feel like a failure. Bihua uses a due count and a calendar. If you stop, the schedule is still there when you return.

There is no server. Backups are files you choose to create. Sync is local Wi-Fi between two devices.

The biggest limitation is verification. The phone path is tested; a current tablet and S Pen pass is still pending. The content gate catches missing readings and dictionary artifacts, not every wrong nuance. The app is not a clinical intervention, and it does not promise literacy.

Using it

  • Write at a size your wrist can control. Change the square in Settings if it feels cramped.
  • Grade the attempt you actually made. If you needed the guide, say so with the grade.
  • Use the guide when you need it, then put it away.
  • Keep sessions short enough to repeat tomorrow.
  • Use words and paper checks as transfer, not as a second job.
  • After a break, use vacation mode and the seven-day ease-back rather than fighting a backlog.
